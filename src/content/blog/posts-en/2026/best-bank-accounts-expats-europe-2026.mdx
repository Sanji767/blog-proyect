# Best Bank Accounts for Expats Living in Europe (2026)

Moving to a new European country means you need a bank account before almost anything else works: salary, rent, utilities, a phone contract and health insurance all ask for an IBAN. The catch is that many traditional banks want local registration first, which you only get after you arrive. In 2026 you can open a working European account from your sofa, before you move — if you pick the right provider. This guide compares the six options that matter most: N26, Revolut, Wise, bunq, Trade Republic and a traditional bank (ING in the Netherlands), on residency rules, ID, English support, IBAN acceptance and multi-currency needs.

## Opening an account before vs. after you move

Timing decides which providers are open to you. **Before you move**, Wise and bunq are the most flexible: Wise opens for residents of most countries with a valid ID, and bunq lets you start signup from anywhere and finish once you move to an EEA country where it operates. Revolut works if you are already legally resident in a supported country — eligibility follows where you legally live, not your citizenship. **Right after arrival, before local registration**, ING in the Netherlands lets you start banking even before you receive your BSN (citizen service number), and bunq gives you 90 days to provide your tax ID. **Once fully registered**, everything opens up, including Trade Republic — which requires you to be a permanent resident of the country where you register and to pay your taxes there.

## N26: the strongest "real bank" option for EU residents

N26 is a fully licensed German bank, regulated by BaFin, with deposits covered up to €100,000 by the German deposit guarantee scheme. For expats who want an account that employers and landlords instantly recognise as a proper bank account, it is the strongest fintech option. The Standard plan is free.

To open an account you must be 18 or older and resident in one of the [24 countries where N26 operates](https://nomadgate.com/open-n26-account/) — most of the EU plus Iceland, Liechtenstein, Norway and Switzerland. You need a mailing address in one of those countries, a valid passport or EU ID card, and a smartphone. Identity verification is usually a selfie plus a photo of your ID in the app. Your tax ID is optional at signup but must be provided eventually.

The limits: N26 is a euro account, not a multi-currency one — you cannot hold US dollar or pound balances. And it no longer operates in the UK. Support is available in English, German, French, Italian and Spanish.

## Revolut: best all-rounder for daily life and travel

Revolut is the most feature-rich option for everyday expat life: a bank account, a card for spending abroad, budgeting tools and savings in one app. In the EEA it operates as Revolut Bank UAB, a Lithuanian bank, so deposits are protected up to €100,000.

Eligibility is broad: Revolut personal accounts are available to legal residents of [39 countries and 10 overseas territories](https://csttimes.com/revolut-supported-countries-2026/), covering the UK, all 30 EEA countries, Switzerland and several non-European markets. What matters is where you legally live, not your passport. You need to be 18 or over, have a smartphone, and verify with a government-issued photo ID and a selfie in the app.

Revolut lets you hold around 38 currencies. The free Standard plan exchanges up to €1,000 a month on weekdays at the interbank rate, then charges a 1% fair-usage fee, and paid plans remove the limit and the weekend markup. Revolut also advertises local account numbers in Sweden, Norway, Denmark and Finland — useful if you are moving to the Nordics.

## Wise: best for international transfers and multi-currency life

Wise is the benchmark for moving money between countries. It uses the mid-market exchange rate with a transparent fee shown upfront — from 0.2% depending on the currency pair — and you can hold more than 40 currencies with local account details in 22 of them, so clients can pay you as if you had a local account. There is no monthly fee.

Opening a Wise account is possible for citizens and residents of most countries with a valid ID. One check to expect: if the ID you verify with was not issued by the same country as the address on your account, Wise may ask for a government-issued document proving your right to live there, such as a residence permit or a long-term visa valid for more than 90 days. Until that check is complete, Wise may limit features such as ordering a card.

The important caveat: Wise Europe SA is a payment institution supervised by the National Bank of Belgium, not a bank. Your money is safeguarded if Wise fails, but there is no deposit guarantee scheme covering it. Use Wise as your international money hub, not as the place where your life savings sit.

## bunq: best for a local IBAN in your new country

bunq is a Dutch bank with a full banking licence, and deposits are covered up to €100,000 by the Dutch Deposit Guarantee Scheme. Its standout feature for expats is local IBANs: you can hold accounts with IBANs from Belgium, Germany, France, Ireland, Spain, Italy or the Netherlands, which ends arguments with landlords and payroll departments that expect a local account number.

You can start signing up from anywhere in the world and finish once you move to an EEA country where bunq operates. You need a valid passport or EU/EEA ID, a selfie for identity verification and an address in the EU or EEA. You do not need a tax ID straight away — bunq gives you up to 90 days to provide it. Note that bunq requires EEA tax residency, not just an address. Plans run from Free (€0, digital card only) to Elite (€18.99/month).

## Trade Republic: best for expats who also want to save and invest

Trade Republic is a German broker-bank combining a current account with investing, paying interest on uninvested cash — 2.50% a year as the standard rate, with a 4.00% promotional rate for new clients on balances up to €20,000 until 1 March 2027 in several countries. It is the strictest of the six to open.

You must be 18 or older, a resident of one of 18 countries — including Germany, France, Spain, Italy, the Netherlands, Belgium, Ireland and Portugal — and you must also pay tax in that country. You need a valid passport or ID card, a mobile number from an EU country, a SEPA bank account to fund it from, and sometimes proof of address. Identity verification is by video call. Since 15 September 2026, Trade Republic has a Belgian branch offering Belgian IBANs — but if you have just arrived and have no tax ID yet, open your everyday account first and come back to Trade Republic once registered.

## ING: the traditional bank that works for expats

ING is the most expat-friendly traditional bank in the Netherlands. Its app and online banking are fully available in English, and you can start banking even before you receive your BSN. ING Go costs €4.00 a month and includes a debit card and a free Orange savings account. ING accepts a valid European passport or ID card; with a non-European passport you need a BSN and a Dutch address within 90 days.

## IBAN discrimination: your foreign IBAN must be accepted

One problem expats hit constantly: an employer, landlord or utility refuses your IBAN because it starts with the "wrong" country code. That is IBAN discrimination, and it is illegal. Article 9 of the SEPA Regulation (EU) No 260/2012 says that a payer making a transfer — and a payee collecting by direct debit — must not specify the Member State where the payment account is located, as long as the account is reachable. Your employer may not require a German IBAN to pay your salary, and your landlord may not require one to collect rent.

Enforcement is the weak point: in February 2026 the Italian Competition Authority fined one company €800,000 for refusing direct debits on non-Italian SEPA IBANs. In practice, a polite message citing Article 9 resolves many cases. If it does not, complain to the national authority — in the Netherlands, that is the Meldpunt IBAN-discriminatie at DNB, which tells you within three weeks whether it takes the case up. Note the rule covers transfers and direct debits only; card payments and payment requests are outside it. If you want to avoid the argument entirely, bunq's local IBANs or a traditional local bank solve it at the source.

One more right worth knowing: under the EU Payment Accounts Directive, every consumer living lawfully in the EU can open a basic euro payment account in their country of residence, whatever their nationality.

## Which account should you open?

Most experienced expats end up with two or three accounts, not one:

- **Open first, before you move:** Wise for multi-currency receiving and cheap transfers, or bunq if you want a local IBAN waiting for you on arrival.
- **Your main European bank:** N26 for a German bank account with full deposit protection, or Revolut for the best everyday app and travel spending.
- **Once settled:** Trade Republic for interest on cash and investing, and a local traditional bank such as ING if you need iDEAL or a branch.
