# IBAN Discrimination in the EU: Legal Rights & How to File Complaints (2026)

When using European digital banks like **Revolut (Lithuanian LT IBAN)**, **N26 (German DE IBAN)**, or **bunq (Dutch NL IBAN)**, you might occasionally encounter an employer or utility provider claiming: *"We only accept local bank account numbers"*.

This practice is known as **IBAN Discrimination** and has been **strictly illegal across the entire European Union since 2014**.

And it is not a rare edge case. The Accept My IBAN campaign has received [5,000 complaints](https://wise.jobs/blog/2025-9/fighting-hidden-fees-and-iban-discrimination) from consumers across Europe, which it passes on to the European Commission and national authorities — and the European Court of Auditors found in 2025 that discrimination based on payment account location persists despite the SEPA Regulation being in force for over a decade, with [France and Spain accounting for the largest shares of reported cases](https://clear.bank/learn/insights/what-is-iban-discrimination-and-how-is-it-being-tackled).

---

## 1. The Legal Basis: SEPA Regulation (EU) No 260/2012

**Article 9 of Regulation (EU) No 260/2012** of the European Parliament explicitly rules that:

> *"A payee accepting a credit transfer or using a direct debit to collect funds from a payer holding a payment account located within the Union shall not specify the Member State in which that payment account is to be located, provided that the payment account is reachable in accordance with Article 3."* ([Regulation (EU) No 260/2012, Article 9(2)](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eur/2012/260/body/adopted?view=plain))

Any business or public authority operating within the SEPA zone accepting credit transfers or direct debits **is legally bound to accept any valid euro IBAN from an account located in the EU or EEA**. SEPA itself now covers [41 countries](https://www.tagada.io/glossary/sepa), after Albania, Moldova, Montenegro, North Macedonia and Serbia joined in 2025–2026.

The European Commission itself identified IBAN discrimination as the main remaining problem with the SEPA Regulation in its [2017 implementation report](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/en/ALL/?uri=CELEX:52017DC0683), noting that cases had decreased but "new cases could still appear". Nearly a decade later, they still do.

### What counts as IBAN discrimination?

- An employer refusing to pay your salary to a non-local IBAN.
- A utility, telecom or insurance company refusing a direct debit from a foreign IBAN.
- A landlord, university or public authority requiring a local account.
- An online form that rejects your IBAN because its country code does not match the expected one.
- A company that *accepts* a foreign IBAN but only under more burdensome conditions — for example, requiring extra paperwork or charging a fee that local IBANs do not pay.

That last point matters: in February 2026, the Italian Competition Authority [fined one company €800,000](https://clear.bank/learn/insights/what-is-iban-discrimination-and-how-is-it-being-tackled) after finding it refused direct debits on non-Italian SEPA IBANs, or accepted them only under more burdensome conditions than Italian IBANs. Unequal treatment is discrimination too, even if the IBAN is technically accepted.

### What is NOT IBAN discrimination?

- A company that does not accept bank transfers or direct debits at all (for example, card-only merchants) is not discriminating — it is refusing a payment method, not an IBAN's country.
- A company asking for an IBAN from a specific country for a genuine, non-payment reason (for example, a local tax form that requires a domestic account for refunds) may still be lawful — but the burden is on them to justify it, and "our system only accepts local IBANs" is not a justification.

## 2. Step-by-Step Resolution Protocol

### Step 1: Send Formal Notice

Reply in writing citing Article 9 of EU Regulation 260/2012. Keep it short, polite and specific:

> "Under Article 9 of Regulation (EU) No 260/2012, a payee accepting credit transfers or direct debits may not specify the Member State in which the payer's payment account is located. My IBAN [LT/DE/NL...] is a valid SEPA IBAN from an account located in the European Union. Please confirm that you will accept it, or explain in writing the legal basis for your refusal."

Written notice matters twice over: it often resolves the issue immediately (many refusals come from front-line staff who simply do not know the rule), and it creates the paper trail you need if you escalate.

### Step 2: Report to National Authorities

File an official complaint with national financial regulators (such as Banco de España, BaFin, or ACPR). Each EU member state has designated a competent authority for SEPA Regulation compliance, and penalties vary widely across the bloc — from minimum fines of €250 to €10,000 and maximum fines from €3,500 to €10 million, depending on the country. Your complaint goes to the authority in the country where the *refusing company* is based, not where your bank is based.

### Step 3: Log via Accept My IBAN

Submit the incident to the EU-wide consumer initiative tracking compliance. Accept My IBAN was launched by Wise in 2021 and is now backed by a coalition of 30 companies and organisations; the European Commission uses its anonymised data as a trusted source for monitoring the problem. Reporting takes a few minutes and helps build the evidence base that drives enforcement — the €800,000 Italian fine above is the kind of outcome that aggregated complaints make possible.

## 3. Practical Workarounds While You Fight It

Your rights are clear, but enforcement takes time — and rent is due this month. Practical options:

- **Open a second account with a local IBAN.** Many digital banks now issue local IBANs by country of residence: N26 issues Spanish IBANs to Spanish residents, Revolut issues local IBANs in France, Ireland, Lithuania, the Netherlands, Romania and Spain for business accounts, and bunq issues Dutch IBANs. Our guide to [choosing a bank in Europe](/en/blog/choose-a-bank-in-europe) explains the local-IBAN question in detail, and our [best digital banks in Europe](/en/blog/best-digital-banks-europe-2026) comparison lists which IBAN each bank issues where.
- **Use the local account for salary and direct debits only.** Keep your main bank for everything else. Two free accounts — one local, one international — is the setup we recommend for students and expats alike; see the [best bank accounts for expats in Europe](/en/blog/best-bank-accounts-expats-europe-2026).
- **Pay by card where a direct debit is refused.** A card payment sidesteps the IBAN question entirely, though it may not work for salary or rent.
- **Escalate in writing, not on the phone.** Phone agents cannot change company policy; a written complaint to the company's legal or compliance team, citing Article 9, usually can.

## FAQ

**My employer says payroll "only supports" local IBANs. Is that legal?**
No. A payroll system's technical limitation is not a legal justification. Article 9 applies to employers exactly as it applies to utilities and public authorities. Send the formal notice in Step 1, addressed to HR or payroll, and escalate to the national authority if they persist.

**Does IBAN discrimination apply to direct debits and credit transfers both?**
Yes. Article 9 covers both: a payee may not specify the Member State of the account for either instrument.

**What if the company is outside the EU?**
Article 9 binds payees within the SEPA zone. A company outside the EU/EEA is not bound by the regulation, though many accept SEPA IBANs voluntarily. If the company has an EU subsidiary or EU operations, the EU entity is bound.

**Can I claim compensation?**
The regulation itself does not set a standard compensation amount for consumers; penalties are set by each member state and paid to the state, not to you. Your practical remedies are acceptance of your IBAN, plus any damages you can show under national consumer law — a consumer association in your country can advise on the specifics.
