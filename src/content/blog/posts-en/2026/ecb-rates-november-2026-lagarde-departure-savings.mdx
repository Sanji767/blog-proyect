# ECB Holds at 2.50% as Inflation Returns — and Lagarde Leaves in February

The European Central Bank has left interest rates unchanged, inflation has returned to its highest level in three years, and the bank's president has announced she will leave early. The next decision, due on 17 December, will set the tone for borrowing costs and deposit rates into the new year — and the data behind it points in an uncomfortable direction.

Rates are on hold, prices are rising again, and the leadership of the central bank is about to change. None of that means a rate rise is certain, or that savers will suddenly earn more on an ordinary account.

## The hold: 2.50% stays in place

The Governing Council held rates unchanged on 29 October 2026. The deposit facility rate remains at 2.50%, in force since 16 September 2026, while the main refinancing rate stands at 2.65% and the marginal lending rate at 2.90%, according to [Euribor Rates](https://euribor-rates.info/en/ecb-interest-rates/).

The deposit rate matters most for savers, because it is what banks earn when they park money at the central bank overnight, and the anchor for the rates they offer on deposits. While it stays at 2.50%, the ceiling for what banks can comfortably pay on savings does not move.

The next decision comes on Thursday 17 December 2026, published at 14:15 CET, with the press conference at 14:45 CET, according to [Euribor Rates](https://euribor-rates.info/en/ecb-interest-rates/). It is the last meeting of the year, and it will be watched closely because the data since October has pointed the wrong way: prices rising again while borrowing costs stay elevated.

## Inflation is back

Euro area inflation was 3.8% in September 2026, its highest level in three years, according to [Immolus](https://www.immolus.com/blog/mortgage-rates-south-tyrol-2026-financing-guide). A central bank that holds rates while inflation rises is, in effect, allowing real rates to fall. If inflation stays high, the case for keeping rates where they are weakens, and the case for raising them strengthens.

The picture is not uniform. Italian inflation stood at 5.4% in October 2026, up from 4.2% in September, according to the same source. A rise of that size in one of the largest economies in the currency bloc is difficult for the Governing Council to ignore.

For savers, 3.8% is the number that matters most. Money in an account paying well below that rate is losing purchasing power every month, even if the balance does not fall. For mortgage holders, the same figure matters for a different reason: if the ECB decides inflation needs a response, borrowing costs could rise rather than fall, and variable-rate mortgages would feel that change first.

## What markets expect in December — and why it is not a decision

After the October meeting, money markets priced roughly a two-in-three chance of a hike to 2.75% in December, according to LSEG data reported by CNBC, with other estimates higher, as reported by [Bravos Estate](http://bravos.estate/blog/ecb-october-2026-hold-euribor-3-239-spain-mortgage). Those are forecasts, not decisions. Markets price probabilities; the Governing Council decides on the data available at the meeting.

A two-in-three chance is not a promise, and not a guarantee that a hike, if it happens, would be the last move or the start of a new tightening cycle. It is a signal that traders, looking at the same inflation data, now think a rise is more likely than a hold or a cut.

The practical approach is to prepare for both outcomes rather than bet on one. If rates rise to 2.75%, the best deposit rates may edge up, but ordinary accounts are unlikely to follow quickly or fully. If rates stay at 2.50%, pressure on variable mortgages eases slightly, but Euribor will still reflect expectations about the future path, not just the current policy rate.

## Lagarde leaves in February

On the same day as the October rate decision, ECB President Christine Lagarde announced that she will step down on 7 February 2027, nine months before her term was due to end in October 2027, according to the [ECB press release](https://www.ecb.europa.eu/press/pr/date/2026/html/ecb.pr261029~abbb6dc64b.en.html). Her successor will be appointed by the European Council, according to [Le Monde](https://www.lemonde.fr/en/economy/article/2026/10/30/at-the-ecb-christine-lagarde-announces-departure-saying-she-wants-freedom-to-engage-on-matters-that-are-not-monetary-policy-matters_6767059_19.html).

Names mentioned in speculation include Klaas Knot, the former Dutch central bank governor, and Pablo Hernández de Cos of the Bank for International Settlements, according to the same report. That speculation is not an appointment, and it is not a policy announcement.

A new president does not automatically mean a new rate path. The Governing Council decides rates collectively, and the president chairs that discussion rather than setting rates alone. The economic data will matter far more than the name of the person chairing the meeting. Lagarde will still be in place for the 17 December decision, so that meeting will be judged on its own merits.

## Most savings accounts still pay far below 2.50%

The gap between the policy rate and what most savers actually earn remains wide. The average euro-area household overnight deposit paid 0.30% in September 2026, against the ECB's 2.50% deposit rate, according to [Copio Capital](https://copiocapital.com/blog/high-yield-savings-accounts-ecb-rate-pass-through). The central bank rate is 2.50%, but the average saver earns a fraction of that.

Banks are not obliged to pass the policy rate through in full, and many do not. An overnight deposit is the cheapest funding for a bank, and banks have little incentive to pay more for it when customers do not move. Inertia is expensive: a saver who leaves money in the same account out of habit is, in effect, lending to the bank at 0.30% on average while the bank can earn 2.50% by placing that money at the ECB.

Fixed-term deposits pay more, but still below the policy rate in many cases. In Germany, new fixed-term deposits of up to one year paid 2.39% in September 2026, according to the same source. That is far better than the average overnight rate, yet still below the deposit facility rate — and, with inflation at 3.8%, even 2.39% is not keeping up with prices.

## Where the better rates actually are

Three current offers show how wide the gap has become. Trade Republic pays new clients 4.07% on balances up to €20,000, with the promotion ending on 1 March 2027, and 2.50% above that, according to [Pollar News](https://pollar.news/en/event/trade-republic-407-savings-promo). Revolut offers new customers 3.51% for four months up to €25,000, according to the same source. Monzo's Spanish savings pot pays 4.00% on up to €5,000 for 12 months, according to the same source.

Each rate is well above the average overnight deposit rate of 0.30% and the German fixed-term average of 2.39%. Each also comes with a limit: the top rate applies to a defined balance, for a defined period, and often only for new customers. A saver with a larger balance will not earn the headline rate on the whole amount, and a saver who stays after the promotional period ends will see the rate fall.

That makes these offers a tool to use deliberately, not a reason to stop checking. Splitting cash across accounts — keeping a limited balance where the top rate applies, and placing the rest where the next-best rate is available — can earn materially more than the average without taking on investment risk. Note the end date of any promotion: the Trade Republic offer ends on 1 March 2027, and a rate of 3.51% that lasts for four months is valuable only if you know when it ends and what replaces it.

With the ECB on hold at 2.50% and inflation at 3.8%, doing nothing is a decision — and a costly one.

## Variable-rate mortgages: Euribor above 3.3%

For mortgage holders, the relevant rate is not the ECB deposit rate but Euribor, and Euribor remains above the policy rate. The 12-month Euribor stood at 3.351% on 18 November 2026, while the 3-month Euribor was 2.825% and the 1-month Euribor was 2.519%, according to [Euribor Rates](https://euribor-rates.info/en/ecb-interest-rates/).

That matters because most variable-rate mortgages in Spain and elsewhere in the euro area are priced off 12-month Euribor. A borrower whose mortgage resets against that benchmark is paying a rate that reflects both the current ECB rate and market expectations about where rates are heading. At 3.351%, a variable mortgage is not cheap, and it will not become cheap simply because the ECB held rates in October.

The official October monthly average of 12-month Euribor, published in Spain's BOE, was 3.239%, slightly below September's 3.247%, according to [Bravos Estate](http://bravos.estate/blog/ecb-october-2026-hold-euribor-3-239-spain-mortgage). The small fall is welcome, but it leaves the average well above 3% — and for a household whose mortgage is reviewed annually, that average is the figure that feeds into the next revision.

The December decision will feed into Euribor, but not one-to-one. If the ECB raises rates to 2.75%, Euribor would be expected to reflect that, and variable mortgages would face upward pressure at their next revision. If the ECB holds at 2.50%, Euribor may ease slightly, but it will still reflect the risk of a later hike.

## What to do before 17 December

First, check the rate on every savings account. If it is close to the average overnight rate of 0.30%, the money is earning far less than the 2.50% policy rate, and far less than the promotional rates available elsewhere. Moving it, or at least part of it, is the single most effective step a saver can take while rates are on hold.

Second, note the end date of any promotional rate, and set a reminder for it. That avoids the common trap of earning a good rate for a few months and then drifting back to a poor one without noticing.

Third, if you have a variable mortgage, check the next revision date and which Euribor tenor it uses. Knowing when the next revision falls turns the December decision from an abstract headline into a concrete date on which a payment may change.

None of these steps depends on whether the ECB hikes in December or holds again. They depend only on the facts as they stand: rates on hold at 2.50%, inflation at 3.8%, Euribor above 3.3%, and a leadership change coming in February. Acting on those facts now is better than waiting for a meeting to decide what to do.
