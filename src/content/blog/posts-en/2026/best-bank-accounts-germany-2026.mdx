# Best Bank Accounts in Germany 2026: Free Accounts, German IBAN and Savings Compared

Germany runs on IBANs. Your employer pays your salary into one, your landlord takes rent from one, and the local tax office, health insurer and mobile provider all expect one. In theory, any euro account with a SEPA IBAN should work everywhere in the EU — IBAN discrimination is illegal. In practice, some German employers and landlords still reject foreign IBANs, so a German IBAN (starting with DE) removes friction a foreign one does not. Our guide to the [best bank accounts for expats in Europe](/en/blog/best-bank-accounts-expats-europe-2026) covers the wider picture; this article focuses on Germany.

There is a second, practical problem: the Anmeldung. Registering your address at the Bürgeramt usually takes days or weeks after you arrive, and you often need a bank account before you can complete the paperwork for a flat or a job. Traditional German banks typically want your Anmeldung, your tax ID and sometimes a SCHUFA credit check before they open anything. The accounts below split into two groups: digital banks you can open from your phone before you register, and established banks that reward you with a genuinely free account once your salary lands in it. According to [MSI Germany's 2026 expat guide](https://msingermany.com/blog/best-german-bank-accounts-expats-2026), N26 Standard is the best first account for most newcomers precisely because it is free, comes with a German IBAN, needs no Anmeldung and works in English.

## Best first account: N26

For someone who has just landed, N26 is the pragmatic answer. [Bankdaten's Germany guide](https://www.bankdaten.de/en/guide/best-bank-account-germany.html) notes that N26 opens in about eight minutes via VideoIdent, and EU citizens do not need an Anmeldung to get started. You get a German IBAN immediately, an app and support in English, and [two free ATM withdrawals per month on the free Standard plan](https://msingermany.com/blog/best-german-bank-accounts-expats-2026). [Expatzentrum's quick picks](https://expatzentrum.de/best-bank-accounts-germany-expats/) reach the same conclusion: N26 is the fast first account, DKB the long-term main account, ING the salary account.

Who it suits: EU citizens and many other passport holders who need a working German IBAN this week, before the Anmeldung appointment, the first payslip or the rental contract. The limits matter too. N26 accepts a defined list of passports and residence permits for VideoIdent, and if your documents are not on it, the account will not open — there is no branch to walk into with extra paperwork. The free plan is deliberately basic: no Girocard (the domestic debit card some German shops, doctors' offices and authorities still prefer), no cash deposits at a counter, and support that is app-based rather than in person. Our full [N26 review for 2026](/en/blog/n26-review-2026) goes through the free plan's costs and limits in detail. Think of N26 as the account that gets you started, not necessarily the one you keep forever.

## Best long-term free account: C24 Bank

If you plan to stay, C24 is the strongest free account in Germany right now — with one catch. [Bankdaten](https://www.bankdaten.de/en/guide/best-bank-account-germany.html) highlights what makes it unusual: a free Mastercard and a free Girocard, 1.00% interest on your balance as of November 2026, sub-accounts that each have their own IBAN, and no minimum income requirement. [How to Germany](https://www.how-to-germany.com/bank-accounts/) confirms the €0 monthly fee and 1% credit interest. In a market where "free" usually means "free if your salary is high enough", C24 is free unconditionally, pays interest on money sitting in the current account, and still gives you the Girocard that digital banks skip.

The catch is language. C24's app, contracts and support are German-first. If your German is still at survival level, you will be translating banking terms on the fly, and a misunderstood fee notice or a blocked card becomes harder to fix. If you are comfortable running your finances in German, C24 is hard to beat as a permanent main account; otherwise, treat it as a second step once you have settled in.

## Established banks: DKB and ING

DKB and ING are the accounts many long-term residents end up with, and both are free only if you meet an income rule. [How to Germany](https://www.how-to-germany.com/bank-accounts/) sets out the terms: DKB is free with €700 or more coming in each month, or if you are under 28; otherwise it costs €4.50 a month. ING is free with €1,000 or more per month, or under 28. [MSI Germany](https://msingermany.com/blog/best-german-bank-accounts-expats-2026) adds the eligibility details: DKB requires EU citizenship or permanent residence and operates in German only, while ING requires an Anmeldung.

Both banks run a SCHUFA check when you open an account — normal in Germany, but it means they are not same-day accounts for someone who arrived yesterday. What you get in return is a full-service German bank: reliable direct debits, a proper credit card option, overdraft facilities, and an IBAN that no employer or landlord will ever question. [Expatzentrum](https://expatzentrum.de/best-bank-accounts-germany-expats/) puts DKB as the long-term main account and ING as the salary account, which matches how most settled expats actually use them: open one once your contract and registration are in place, move your salary in, and the account stays free.

## If you want branches: Commerzbank

Some people want a human being and a counter — for cash deposits, certified documents, or simply reassurance. Commerzbank is the realistic branch option for newcomers: [How to Germany](https://www.how-to-germany.com/bank-accounts/) lists its digital GiroKonto at €4.90 a month for new customers, with the StartKonto free for customers under 28. [Expatzentrum](https://expatzentrum.de/best-bank-accounts-germany-expats/) picks Commerzbank specifically for branch support. You pay for that network, and €4.90 a month is €58.80 a year for something the digital banks give you free — so it only makes sense if you will actually use the branches. For comparison, [MSI Germany's table](https://msingermany.com/blog/best-german-bank-accounts-expats-2026) puts Deutsche Bank at €6.90 a month and Sparkasse accounts at €4–12, which is why neither makes this shortlist for a cost-conscious newcomer.

## Saving and investing add-on: Trade Republic

Trade Republic is not really a current account competitor — it is a broker that now behaves like one. [How to Germany](https://www.how-to-germany.com/bank-accounts/) lists it at €0 a month with 2.5% interest on cash, and [Expatzentrum](https://expatzentrum.de/best-bank-accounts-germany-expats/) recommends it as a saving and investing add-on rather than a main account. That 2.5% on uninvested cash is well above what any German current account pays, and you get a German IBAN and a Visa debit card. The limits are real, though: no overdraft and no joint accounts, according to [How to Germany](https://www.how-to-germany.com/bank-accounts/). Use it the way the guides suggest — keep your salary and direct debits in a proper Girokonto, and park spare cash in Trade Republic where it earns 2.5% instead of nothing.

## Second accounts: Wise and Revolut

Wise and Revolut are excellent second accounts and risky only accounts. [MSI Germany's comparison](https://msingermany.com/blog/best-german-bank-accounts-expats-2026) is explicit about why: Wise is free but gives you a Belgian IBAN, not a German one, and Revolut gives a German IBAN only if you are a German resident. [Expatzentrum](https://expatzentrum.de/best-bank-accounts-germany-expats/) recommends Wise for international transfers and warns it is not a full Girokonto replacement. If you send money home or get paid in another currency, Wise's exchange rates and Revolut's multi-currency features beat every German bank on this page. But a Belgian IBAN is precisely the kind that occasionally trips up a German employer's payroll system or a landlord's direct debit — so keep your German salary and rent on a DE IBAN and use Wise or Revolut alongside it.

## How to open an account, step by step

1. **Choose by timing, not by brand.** If you need an IBAN before your Anmeldung, start with N26 or C24. If you already have a job contract and registration, DKB or ING will likely serve you better long term.
2. **Prepare your documents.** A valid passport or EU ID card, your German address (even a temporary one for the digital banks), and — for DKB and ING — your Anmeldung and tax ID once you have them.
3. **Verify your identity.** Digital banks use VideoIdent: a short video call where you show your ID and confirm your details. [Bankdaten](https://www.bankdaten.de/en/guide/best-bank-account-germany.html) puts N26's whole process at about eight minutes.
4. **Activate and test.** Set up the app, order or activate the card, and make a small test transfer before you give the IBAN to your employer.
5. **Move the money flows.** Give the DE IBAN to payroll, set up rent and utilities as direct debits (Lastschrift), and check the first month carefully — if you chose DKB or ING, confirm your incoming salary clears the €700 or €1,000 threshold so the account stays free.

## Verdict: which account for which situation

- **Just landed, no Anmeldung yet:** N26 Standard. Free, German IBAN, English, open in minutes. Upgrade or switch later.
- **Settled, with a salary and some German:** C24 if you want the best free package (Girocard plus 1% interest), or DKB/ING if you prefer an established bank and your income clears their thresholds.
- **Student or under 28:** almost everything is free for you — DKB and ING waive their income rules under 28, and Commerzbank's StartKonto is free. Pick on language and features, not price.
- **You need branches or cash services:** Commerzbank at €4.90 a month, accepting that you are paying for the network.
- **You have spare cash to park:** add Trade Republic for 2.5% on cash, alongside — not instead of — your main account.
- **You move money internationally:** add Wise as a second account, and keep the German IBAN for salary and rent.

The practical sequence most expats end up following is simple: open N26 on arrival, then move to C24, DKB or ING once the Anmeldung, contract and first salary are in place. Fees, rates and conditions change, so confirm the current terms on the bank's own site before you apply — and check our [guide to expat bank accounts across Europe](/en/blog/best-bank-accounts-expats-europe-2026) if your move might take you beyond Germany.
