# Best Bank Accounts for Teens in Europe (2026): 7 Tested Picks for Kids & Parents

More and more teenagers are opening their own bank accounts to receive allowance money, save for goals, make online purchases, or manage their first part-time earnings. However, not all accounts are tailored for minors, and conditions vary significantly across European banks — and some well-known fintechs, such as N26, do not accept anyone under 18 at all.

## What Is a Teen Bank Account?

A youth account is designed specifically for minors who have not yet reached legal adulthood (typically between **6 and 17 years old**, depending on the provider). A parent or legal guardian opens and supervises the account; the child cannot open one alone.

Key features typically include:

- **Receiving money:** automated allowance or family transfers.
- **Debit card:** physical and virtual contactless cards (see our [best travel cards guide](/en/blog/best-no-fx-fee-travel-cards-2026)).
- **Online and in-store purchases:** with parent-supervised 3D Secure verification.
- **ATM withdrawals:** with adjustable daily limits.
- **Savings pockets / goals:** visual savings trackers; estimate growth with our **[Savings Yield Calculator](/en/herramientas/interes-compuesto)**.
- **Parental controls:** real-time push alerts, spending limits, and 1-tap card locking.
- **Financial education:** practical budgeting skills; the [European Central Bank (ECB)](https://www.ecb.europa.eu/) supports financial literacy initiatives across the euro area.

One thing teen accounts do **not** offer is credit: no overdraft, no credit card, and no autonomous investing or crypto trading. That is by design — a teen account can only spend money that is actually in it.

## The 7 Best Teen Bank Accounts in Europe

### 1. Revolut <18 — Best app-first account (ages 6–17)

[Revolut <18](https://www.revolut.com/en-PL/revolut-under-18-benefits-parents-and-guardians/) is the most widely available teen account in Europe. It is open to children aged **6 to 17**, and it is **free for one child** — you only need to upgrade if you want accounts for multiple kids. The parent must hold a Revolut account and acts as the lead parent; children aged 15 or under are set up from the parent's app, while 16- and 17-year-olds can start the process themselves with parental approval.

The standard card is free (delivery fees may apply), and customised card designs cost extra. Features include chores and allowance scheduling, spending limits, instant notifications, and savings goals. Teens aged 16+ can also send payments to other Revolut <18 users. The <18 account is a sub-account of the parent's Revolut account, so it follows the parent's plan fees and limits. See our full [Revolut review](/en/blog/revolut-review-2026) and the [Revolut vs N26 comparison](/en/comparar/revolut-vs-n26).

**Watch out:** the free tier covers one child only, and foreign-exchange allowances for the teen account are lower than the parent's.

### 2. Pixpay — Best for France (ages 8+)

[Pixpay](https://www.wealthadviser.co/best-teen-debit-card-2026-comparison/) is a French teen card and app for children **from age 8**, now part of the GoHenry group after its 2022 acquisition. It costs **€3.99 a month**, with higher plans up to €9.99 a month, and no commitment. The card is a Mastercard debit with mandatory authorisation, so no overdraft is possible. It works in-store, online and abroad, supports Apple Pay and Google Pay, and includes instant card locking, adjustable limits, real-time notifications and an educational app with budget missions.

Pixpay is not a bank: it operates as an e-money distributor through Treezor, an ACPR-licensed e-money institution in the Société Générale group, so balances are held in safeguarding accounts rather than covered by a deposit guarantee scheme.

**Watch out:** Pixpay is built for France. If your family does not live there, Revolut <18 is the more practical pick.

### 3. GoHenry — Best lessons, but UK only (ages 6–18)

GoHenry is the pioneer of kids' money apps, with a prepaid Visa debit card and an app built around financial education: automated allowances, chore lists, savings goals, gift links for relatives, and more than 100 short "Money Missions" lessons. It accepts children aged **6 to 18**. Pricing is **£3.99 a month per child** on Everyday, **£5.99 on Plus** (unlimited free top-ups and interest on savings goals at 2.63% AER, variable), or **£9.99 a month for up to four children** on Max. There is a one-month free trial on each plan. Everyday includes one free top-up a month, then 50p per top-up.

Two important caveats: GoHenry is **UK-only** — the parent must be 18 or over and live in the UK, and cards are only posted to UK addresses. And GoHenry is **not a bank**: its money is electronic money held in safeguarded accounts, not covered by the FSCS the way a bank deposit is. Barclays completed its acquisition of GoHenry's UK business on 1 October 2026.

**Watch out:** at £3.99 a month per child, GoHenry costs £47.88 a year per child — the most expensive option here for a single child. The lessons are the reason to pay; if you only need a card and controls, a free bank account does the same job.

### 4. ABN AMRO Young Person's Growth Account — Best Dutch bank account (ages 0–18)

The [ABN AMRO Young Person's Growth Account](https://www.abnamro.nl/en/personal/payments/youth-account/index.html) is a current account in the child's name for ages **0 to 18**, and it is **completely free** — no monthly fees, and the debit card is free too. The parent needs an ABN AMRO current account of their own, and the child's BSN (citizen service number) is required to open it. Parents decide what the child can and cannot do; contactless payment is available from age 16, with a maximum of €50 per payment and €100 in total for younger users. ABN AMRO also includes free access to the Gimi money app for children aged 8 to 13, and was offering a €15 pocket-money gift for accounts opened before 1 January 2027.

**Watch out:** this is a Dutch product — it requires Dutch residency paperwork (BSN) and a parent account at ABN AMRO.

### 5. ING Kinderrekening / Jongerenrekening — Best Dutch all-rounder (ages 0–17)

ING splits its youth offer by age: the **Kinderrekening** is a current account for children aged **0 to 11**, including an Oranje Spaarrekening savings account; at 12 it becomes the free **Jongerenrekening** for ages **12 to 17**, also with a savings account included; at 18 it converts into a student account. Opening requires the child's date of birth and BSN, and — for under-18s — a visit to an ING branch with the legal representative present. There is no way to open it if the legal representative is not present.

**Watch out:** the branch visit is a real hurdle compared with the app-only sign-up at Revolut <18 or Pixpay.

### 6. BoursoBank Freedom — Best free French bank account (ages 12–17)

BoursoBank (the online bank of the Société Générale group) offers a free account for teens aged **12 to 17** with its own IBAN and a free Visa debit card with systematic authorisation, so no overdraft is possible. Payments have a default limit of €200 over 30 days (which can be raised to €5,000) and withdrawals up to €100 over 7 days (up to €500). Parents manage limits and visibility from their own app, and at least one parent must already be a BoursoBank client. Unlike the prepaid apps, this is a real bank account with a French IBAN.

**Watch out:** the parent-must-be-a-client rule means opening a teen account also means opening a parent account first if you are not already with BoursoBank.

### 7. Banxup — Best for Société Générale families (ages 10+)

Banxup is Société Générale's teen offer, available **from age 10** and **free when a parent holds an account at Société Générale**. It covers online and in-store payments and withdrawals in France and abroad, with parental controls in the app. It is the simplest choice if the family already banks with Société Générale and wants a teen card without a new provider or a monthly fee.

**Watch out:** like the other French bank offers, it is tied to one banking group and one country.

## Neobanks vs Traditional Banks for Youth

App-first accounts (Revolut <18, Pixpay, GoHenry) win on features: chores, allowance automation, instant notifications and gamified savings are built in from day one, and sign-up takes minutes. Traditional bank youth accounts (ABN AMRO, ING, BoursoBank, Banxup) win on price — most are free — and on protection: the money sits in a real bank account covered by the national deposit guarantee scheme up to €100,000, whereas prepaid e-money apps hold funds in safeguarding accounts without that guarantee.

Note that N26, often mentioned alongside Revolut, is **not** an option for minors: you must be over 18 to open an N26 account. Families who bank with N26 need a separate provider for their children — Revolut <18 is the closest equivalent.

## How to Choose a Teen Bank Account

1. **Start with your country.** Most free youth accounts are national: ABN AMRO and ING need Dutch residency, BoursoBank and Banxup need a French parent account, GoHenry needs a UK address. Revolut <18 is the only option here that works across most of Europe.
2. **Check the real cost.** Free bank accounts cost €0 a year. Pixpay costs about €48 a year; GoHenry £47.88 a year per child on its cheapest plan. Ask whether the lessons and chores features are worth that to you.
3. **Match the age.** A 7-year-old needs a simple card and allowance automation; a 16-year-old with a part-time job needs a real IBAN for salary payments and higher limits. Revolut <18 (from 6) and ABN AMRO (from 0) cover the youngest; Banxup starts at 10 and BoursoBank at 12.
4. **Check who holds the money.** A bank account is covered by the deposit guarantee up to €100,000; an e-money app is not. For pocket money the difference rarely matters, but for larger savings it does.
5. **Look at the controls, not the marketing.** Every option here offers spending limits and card freezing. The differences are in chores, allowance scheduling, savings goals and financial lessons — decide which of those your family will actually use.

## Downsides and Honest Notes

- **Monthly fees add up.** A €3.99 or £3.99 monthly subscription is roughly €48 a year per child — more than many teens will ever hold in the account. Free bank accounts avoid this entirely.
- **Prepaid apps are not banks.** GoHenry and Pixpay hold money as e-money in safeguarding accounts. That is safe in normal operation, but it is not deposit insurance.
- **Age rules vary by country.** Revolut <18's payment features between teens, for example, have country-specific minimum ages, and 16+ is the norm.
- **No interest to speak of.** Teen accounts are spending and learning tools, not savings products. GoHenry Plus pays 2.63% AER (variable) on savings goals, but most options pay nothing.
- **Accounts end at 18.** Every youth account converts or closes at adulthood. Check what it becomes — ING converts to a student account; Revolut <18 users move to a standard adult account — so the transition is planned, not a surprise.

## 5 Essential Tips for Parents

1. **Automate allowance:** set a fixed weekly or monthly transfer so teens learn to budget independently.
2. **Teach the 50/30/20 rule:** 50% for needs, 30% for wants, and 20% for savings — including a starter safety net (see our **[emergency fund blueprint](/en/blog/how-to-build-emergency-fund-guide-2026)**).
3. **Monthly spending reviews:** review the app's visual spending breakdowns together constructively.
4. **Beware of subscriptions:** audit recurring app and gaming subscriptions regularly.
5. **Digital security:** remind teens never to share PINs, passwords, or SMS verification codes (read our **[online banking security guide](/en/blog/how-to-protect-bank-account-phishing-2026)**).

## Conclusion

A teen bank account is more than just a place to store pocket money — it is a powerful tool to build **real financial confidence and lifelong responsible habits**. For most European families, **Revolut <18** is the easiest starting point: free for one child, available from age 6, and working across borders. If you live in the Netherlands or France, your own bank's free youth account is usually the better deal. And if your priority is financial lessons rather than a card, GoHenry justifies its fee — but only if you live in the UK.
