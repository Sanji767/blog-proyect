# Best Savings Accounts for Kids in Europe (2026)

Saving for a child is a different job from giving a teenager spending money. A teen account is about a card, limits and learning to budget. A savings account for a child is about interest, time and parental control: money that compounds for years, in the child's name, that they cannot spend on impulse — and that you cannot quietly dip into either.

There is no single pan-European kids' savings account. What exists is a patchwork: a generous tax wrapper in the UK, a state-set tax-free account in France, and a new generation of kids' accounts from German neobrokers that pay real interest on cash. This guide compares them with rates verified in November 2026, and explains how to choose based on where you live.

## Junior Cash ISA (UK): the best deal in Europe for kids' savings

If you live in the UK, the Junior Cash ISA is the strongest children's savings product in Europe. You can pay in up to £9,000 per child in the 2026/27 tax year, all interest is free of UK tax, and the money is locked away until the child turns 18 — which is exactly what you want for long-term saving, and exactly what makes it unsuitable for money you might need back.

The best rates as of 19 November 2026, according to MoneySavingExpert: Skipton Building Society pays 4.00% AER variable (open by post or in branch only), while the best accounts you can open online are government-owned NS&I at 3.90% and app-based Triodos Bank at 3.79%. Bath Building Society advertises 4.15%, but only for existing account holders or people who live, work or study in Bath. The £9,000 annual limit is frozen until April 2031, and deposits are protected up to £120,000 per person by the Financial Services Compensation Scheme (a limit raised on 1 December 2025).

The lock-in deserves a moment's thought. A Junior ISA cannot be touched until the child's 18th birthday, and at 18 the money becomes theirs outright — you cannot attach conditions. If you are comfortable with that, nothing else in this guide beats a tax-free 4%.

## Livret A (France): tax-free saving from birth

France's Livret A is the most widely held savings product in the country — over 56 million accounts — and it can be opened for a child from birth with a parent's signature. The rate is set by the state and reviewed twice a year: since 1 August 2026 it pays 1.70% net, tax-free, and that rate is fixed until at least 31 January 2027. Interest is fully exempt from income tax and social charges, deposits are capped at €22,950 per person, and the money is available at any time.

The rate is modest next to a Junior ISA, but the Livret A has two advantages no other account in this guide matches: it is open to minors and non-residents alike, and each family member can hold one, so a family of four can shelter up to €91,800 tax-free across four accounts. For children aged 12 to 25 there is also the Livret Jeune, with a €1,600 ceiling and a rate set by each bank but never lower than the Livret A's 1.70%.

The trade-off is access: unlike a Junior ISA, Livret A money is not locked away. Parents control withdrawals until the child is 16, but the temptation to dip in is entirely on you.

## Scalable Capital Kids (Germany): up to 3.00% fixed for children

Scalable Capital launched children's accounts in Germany in October 2025, and they are the most straightforward way for German parents to earn real interest in a child's name. Parents can open an Overnight or Fixed-term savings account directly in their children's names as part of the Scalable Kids account: the money legally belongs to the child, while the parents manage it through their own app.

Rates from 1 September 2026: the flexible Overnight account pays 2.60% p.a. (variable) on unlimited balances, and Fixed-term accounts pay 2.75% for 12 months or 3.00% for 24 months, starting from €1 with no maximum. Interest on the child's savings is taxable in Germany, but each child has a €1,000 annual saver's tax-free allowance (Sparer-Pauschbetrag), which covers the interest on a substantial balance at these rates. Scalable's own survey found that 70% of German parents still rely on classic savings accounts such as overnight or fixed-term accounts when saving for their children — this is that habit, with a competitive rate attached.

## Trade Republic Child Savings Account: the widest reach

Trade Republic launched its Child Savings Account in Germany in May 2025 and has since extended it to France and Spain, with availability now across Germany, Austria, France, Spain, Italy, the Netherlands, Belgium, Ireland and Portugal — the widest footprint of any account in this guide. Parents open the account on behalf of their children in a few minutes in the app, and it includes free ETF savings plans, fractional trading and the full ECB interest rate passed through on the entire cash balance, uncapped. Trade Republic's standard rate on cash is 2.50% a year (2.53% AER) as of September 2026.

Two features make it stand out for long-term saving. First, "Trade Republic Kindergeld": fund fees for selected Vanguard ETFs, such as the FTSE All-World, are refunded monthly and automatically reinvested until the child turns 18. Second, parents can use the child's annual tax allowance of up to €13,000 in Germany. The account is in the child's name and transfers to them fully at 18. If you want to combine cash savings with a monthly ETF plan from the same account — for example, investing the monthly Kindergeld of €255 per child — this is the most complete option available in continental Europe.

One caution: Trade Republic's child account is a brokerage account as much as a savings account. The cash interest is real, but the product is designed to lead you towards investing, and investments can fall as well as rise. If you want pure savings with no market risk, the Scalable fixed-term account or a Livret A is the cleaner choice.

## What about the rest of Europe?

Outside the UK, France and Germany, dedicated kids' savings accounts are thin on the ground, and that is worth saying plainly rather than padding this guide with unverified options.

- **Ireland:** State Savings offers a way to save Child Benefit and earn tax-free interest after five years, and Irish banks offer children's demand deposit accounts — but Raisin, the main pan-European savings marketplace, states plainly that it does not offer children's savings accounts.
- **The Netherlands:** children's investments are added to the parent's Box 3 wealth-tax base, and Saxo offers an account for minors in the Netherlands only, managed by a parent until the child turns 18 — but that is a brokerage account, not a savings account.
- **Germany's Frühstart-Rente:** under the coalition agreement, children from the age of 6 are planned to receive a government contribution of €10 per month into a retirement savings account. It is not yet live, but both Trade Republic and Scalable Capital have built their kids' accounts to be ready for it.
- **Teen spending accounts are not savings accounts.** Products such as Revolut <18 are designed for pocket money and card spending with parental controls — useful, but they are not where long-term savings belong. We cover them separately in our guide to teen bank accounts.

If your country has no dedicated kids' savings product, the practical fallback is a savings account in your own name earmarked for your child, or a Trade Republic child account if your country is on its list.

## How to choose

- **Live in the UK?** Open a Junior Cash ISA first. Tax-free interest at 4.00% with a £9,000 annual allowance is the best children's savings deal in Europe, and the lock-in until 18 is a feature, not a bug.
- **Live in France?** Open a Livret A for each child from birth. At 1.70% tax-free it will not make anyone rich, but it is simple, state-guaranteed and every family member can hold one.
- **Live in Germany?** Scalable Capital Kids for pure savings (2.60% flexible or 3.00% fixed for two years), or Trade Republic's Child Savings Account if you also want to invest monthly into ETFs with refunded fund fees.
- **Live elsewhere in the eurozone?** Check whether Trade Republic's child account is available in your country — it covers nine markets. Otherwise, use a high-yield savings account in your own name and keep the child's money mentally (and ideally physically) separate.

Whichever you choose, the habit matters more than the rate: a small monthly amount started at birth beats a large lump sum started at 15. The rate decides how fast the pot grows; starting early decides whether there is a pot at all.
