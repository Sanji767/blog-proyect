# Best Bank Accounts for Families in Europe 2026: Kids' Accounts, Joint Accounts and Family Savings

A family does not need one bank account. It needs three things that work together: a safe way for children to learn to spend, a shared pot for the household, and somewhere the family's money actually earns interest instead of sitting idle. Get those three right and the banking setup disappears into the background.

This guide compares the family banking options that work across Europe in 2026, with rates and fees verified in November 2026: kids' accounts from the big neobanks, custodial savings for children, and the joint accounts parents use to run the household. For the kids' savings angle, see our [guide to the best savings accounts for kids in Europe](/en/blog/best-savings-accounts-kids-europe-2026), and for teenagers, our [guide to the best bank accounts for teens and kids](/en/blog/best-bank-accounts-teens-kids-europe-2026).

## Revolut <18: the best all-rounder for most families

Revolut <18 is the most complete kids' account in Europe, and the one most families should look at first. It is available for children aged 6 to 17, and free with a parent's Revolut account: the Standard plan includes one kids' account, while paid plans allow up to five accounts plus a co-parent, according to [Revolut's own parents' guide](https://www.revolut.com/en-FR/revolut-kids-and-teens-parent-and-guardians/). Parents set spending limits, approve or block transactions, and see every purchase in real time.

The savings side is genuinely useful. The Kids & Teens Instant Access Savings account pays up to 2,75% per year, variable, paid daily, with the rate depending on the parent's plan — from 2,5% on Standard to 2,75% on Ultra — and a maximum deposit of €4,200, per [Revolut's kids' account page](https://www.revolut.com/en-DK/kids-and-teens/kids-bank-account/). Deposits are covered by Lithuanian deposit insurance. The basic card is free; custom designs cost €4,99–€19,99.

The €4,200 cap is the main limitation: enough for pocket money and birthday money compounding over a few years, but not a place to park a large gift from grandparents. For the full picture on Revolut's plans and fees, read our [Revolut review for 2026](/en/blog/revolut-review-2026). For a family that already uses Revolut, <18 is the default choice: no monthly fee for the kids' account, real interest on savings, and parental controls that work.

## Trade Republic custodial saving: the simplest place for a child's savings to earn

Trade Republic's custodial saving for children takes a different approach: no card and no spending app for the child, just a savings account in the child's name that earns 2,5% on uninvested cash, according to the [Freenance junior savings ranking](https://freenance.io/rankings/best-junior-savings-account-europe-2026/), which read the rate at the issuer on 4 November 2026. It is available in Germany, Austria, France, Spain, Italy, the Netherlands, Belgium, Ireland and Portugal.

That simplicity is the point. A custodial account is money the parents manage for the child until they come of age — no card to lose, no impulse spending, no monthly fee to justify. The 2,5% rate is lower than bunq's headline figure, but it applies to uninvested cash with instant access, and there is no deposit cap mentioned in the sources we checked. For grandparents' gifts, birthday money and any lump sum that should quietly compound, it is one of the cleanest options available. Our [Trade Republic review for 2026](/en/blog/trade-republic-review-2026) covers the platform in full.

One caveat: check in the app whether the rate still applies in your country, because Trade Republic adjusts its rate when the European Central Bank moves.

## bunq Child Account: the highest rate, but only on a paid plan

bunq's Child Account pays 3,11% until the first threshold reset, then 1,61% on the first €10,000 up to the threshold and 3,11% above it, according to the [Freenance ranking](https://freenance.io/rankings/best-junior-savings-account-europe-2026/). It is available in 30 EEA countries. On paper, that is the highest rate for kids' savings on this list — but the parent must be on a paid bunq plan, costing €3,99–€18,99 per month.

At the cheapest plan, €3,99 a month is €47,88 a year. On a small balance, that fee eats the extra interest entirely: the gap between bunq's 3,11% and Trade Republic's 2,5% is 0,61 percentage points, which on €4,200 of savings is about €25,62 a year — less than the cheapest plan costs. bunq only wins on larger balances, or for parents who already pay for bunq. If you would subscribe just for the child's rate, do the calculation first — and check in the app how the threshold reset works.

## N26 Spaces for Kids: savings pots inside the parent's account

N26 Spaces for Kids works differently: instead of a separate account in the child's name, parents create savings spaces for children aged 0 to 17 inside their own N26 account, available in 24 EEA countries. The interest rate ranges from 1,26% to 2,80%, depending on the parent's plan — Smart, You or Metal — according to the [Freenance ranking](https://freenance.io/rankings/best-junior-savings-account-europe-2026/).

The advantage is that the child gets a named pot from birth — useful for the earliest years, when a card would be pointless anyway — and the parent keeps full control. The disadvantage is that the child never really owns the account: it is a space inside the parent's banking, and the rate depends on paying for a higher-tier plan. For families already on N26 You or Metal, Spaces for Kids is a free and sensible way to organise savings per child. For families on the free plan, the 1,26% rate is the lowest on this list. Check in the app which rate your plan gives you.

## Pixpay and GoHenry: education first, interest second

Not every family wants a savings rate. Some want a teaching tool: a card for the child, chores and allowances in the app, and spending limits the parent controls. That is where Pixpay and GoHenry fit. Pixpay costs €3,99–€9,99 per month, covers ages 8 to 18, and is available in France, Spain and Italy. GoHenry costs £3,99–£9,99 per month for up to four children, covers ages 6 to 18, and is available in the UK only.

Neither product states an interest rate on savings, which tells you what they are for: learning to spend, not earning interest. The monthly fee buys the educational features — tasks, allowances, real-time notifications, card controls. But if the goal is to make the child's money grow, pair one of these with a savings account from the earlier sections rather than treating the card as the savings vehicle. A child can learn to budget on a Pixpay card while the birthday money earns 2,5% at Trade Republic; the two jobs do not have to live in the same app.

## Joint accounts for parents: the shared pot that runs the household

A kids' account solves the children's money. The household still needs a shared pot: rent or mortgage, groceries, utilities, school costs, holidays. That is the job of a joint account. Our [guide to the best joint bank accounts for couples in Europe](/en/blog/best-joint-bank-accounts-couples-europe-2026) covers the options in detail, including which accounts give both partners full access and what happens to the money if the relationship ends. The setup that works for most families is simple: a joint account for all shared spending, funded by a standing order from each partner's personal account on payday. The joint account is for spending; the savings accounts are for the future. Mixing the two is how emergency funds get spent on groceries.

## Family savings strategy: emergency fund first, then the kids

The order matters more than the rate. Before optimising a child's savings account, the family needs its own emergency fund: three to six months of essential expenses in an instantly accessible account. Our [guide to building an emergency fund](/en/blog/how-to-build-emergency-fund-guide-2026) walks through the calculation. Only once that is funded does it make sense to chase the best rate on the children's money. 

After the emergency fund, the next pot is the kids' savings — birthday money, gifts from relatives, a small monthly contribution — in one of the accounts above. Then there is timing: the ECB's deposit rate stands at 2,50%, with a hike to 2,75% expected on 17 December 2026, according to [Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/markets/europe/ecb-policy-direction-clear-december-uncertain-after-sleijpen-says-2026-11-24/). If that hike happens, savings rates should edge up with it — an argument for keeping the family's main savings in flexible accounts now rather than locking money into a fixed term.

The amounts are small in the early years, and that is fine. A child who watches €500 grow at 2,5% learns more about money than a child whose parents optimised the last 0,5% of interest. The habit matters more than the rate.

## Verdict: which setup fits your family

For most families, the default setup is Revolut <18: free with a parent's account, a real card for the child, parental controls that work, and up to 2,75% on savings up to €4,200. If you have larger sums to save for a child and do not need a card, Trade Republic's custodial account at 2,5% is the simplest home for the money. bunq's Child Account wins on rate at 3,11%, but only if a parent already pays for a bunq plan — otherwise the fee cancels the advantage. N26 Spaces for Kids suits families already on N26's paid plans.

And if what you really want is a financial education tool rather than a savings rate, Pixpay in France, Spain or Italy — or GoHenry in the UK — is the honest choice, paired with a separate savings account so the money still earns. Keep it simple: one joint account for the household, one account per child, one emergency fund for the family.
