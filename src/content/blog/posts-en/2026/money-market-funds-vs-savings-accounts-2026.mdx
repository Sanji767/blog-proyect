# Money Market Funds vs Savings Accounts (2026): Which Pays More?

With evolving central bank interest rate policies across Europe, savers and investors are looking for optimal cash management strategies. Two of the most popular low-risk options in 2026 are **high-yield savings accounts** and **Money Market Funds (MMFs)**.

While both instruments offer exceptional capital safety and liquidity, they differ significantly in tax treatment, maximum balance limits, and underlying asset structures.

The short answer for most people in late 2026: a high-yield savings account currently pays more than a euro money market fund, with stronger protection and instant access. MMFs win in specific situations — large balances above savings-account caps, multi-currency cash, and money held inside a brokerage account. The rest of this guide shows the numbers behind that verdict.

## What is a Money Market Fund and How Does It Work?

A **Money Market Fund** is a specialized mutual fund investing exclusively in short-term, highly liquid debt instruments with an average maturity of less than 60 days.

Key underlying assets include:
1. **Short-Term Government Debt:** Treasury bills issued by AAA-rated sovereign nations like Germany, France, or Netherlands.
2. **High-Grade Commercial Paper:** Short-term promissory notes issued by top-tier European banks.

Because of the ultra-short duration, MMFs do not experience the price fluctuations associated with long-term bonds. Their Net Asset Value (NAV) grows steadily on a daily basis.

### The scale of the market

Money market funds are not a niche product. European MMF net assets [reached €2.1 trillion at the end of 2025](https://cranedata.com/archives/all-articles/11400/), up from €2.0 trillion in 2024, according to EFAMA's 2026 Fact Book — with €145 billion of net sales in 2025 alone. The offshore (Ireland- and Luxembourg-domiciled) segment tracked by Crane Data hit a [record $1.748 trillion in November 2026](https://k8.cranedata.com/archives/news/2026/11/). Most of that money is institutional, but retail access has grown fast through brokers and fintech apps that sweep idle cash into MMFs automatically.

A typical euro MMF portfolio holds around 39% commercial paper, 23% certificates of deposit, 20% repo and 4% treasury securities, according to [Crane Data's holdings analysis](https://cranedata.com/archives/all-articles/11253/) — diversified across France, Japan, the US, Canada, the Netherlands and Germany. That diversification is the safety story: no single issuer can sink the fund.

### The yield reality in late 2026

Here is where the comparison turns against MMFs for most savers. Euro-denominated MMFs yielded [1.92% on average in March 2026](https://cranedata.com/archives/all-articles/11253/), according to Crane Data — below the ECB deposit facility rate of 2.50%, because fund fees (typically 0.10–0.20% a year) and the mix of short-term paper drag the net yield below the policy rate. Meanwhile, the best high-yield savings accounts pass on close to the full ECB rate: Trade Republic pays 2.50% on cash balances.

On €50,000, that gap is real money:

| | Rate | Annual interest on €50,000 |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| High-yield savings account | 2.50% | €1,250 |
| Euro MMF (average) | ~1.92% | ~€960 |
| **Difference** | 0.58 pp | **~€290/year** |

The MMF yield moves with the ECB rate, so the gap narrows or widens as policy changes — but the structural drag of fund fees means a savings account paying the full ECB rate will usually win on yield alone. The ECB [held the deposit rate at 2.50% on 29 October 2026](https://www.ecb.europa.eu/press/pr/date/2026/html/ecb.mp261029~9a493adf17.en.html), with the next decision on 17 December 2026.

## The Protection Gap: DGS vs. Investment Risk

This is the most important difference, and the one most often glossed over.

A savings account at an EU bank is covered by a deposit guarantee scheme up to **€100,000 per depositor per bank** under [Directive 2014/49/EU](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/en/LSU/?uri=CELEX:32014L0049), with repayment within 7 working days since 2024. If the bank fails, the scheme pays you back.

A money market fund is an investment, not a deposit. It is **not** covered by any deposit guarantee scheme. If the fund's assets lose value, your investment loses value — there is no government backstop. In practice, euro MMFs are extremely stable: EU money market fund regulation (Regulation (EU) 2017/1131) imposes strict rules on portfolio quality, maturity, diversification and liquidity, and requires funds to hold weekly maturing assets. The main fund types are:

- **LVNAV (low-volatility NAV) funds:** the most common retail type in Europe. They aim to keep a stable €1 share price but can reprice if the market value deviates too far.
- **VNAV (variable NAV) funds:** the share price moves with the market value of the portfolio, usually by tiny amounts.
- **Public debt CNAV funds:** invest only in government debt and maintain a constant €1 price; the most conservative type.

No euro MMF has "broken the buck" in the way some US funds did in 2008, and the post-2008 regulation was designed precisely to prevent it. But "extremely unlikely" is not the same as "guaranteed", and the distinction matters for money you cannot afford to lose — which is why your emergency fund belongs in a savings account, not a fund. ## Tax: MMFs Are Taxed Differently

Interest from a savings account is taxed as interest income in most countries. Returns from an MMF are typically taxed as fund income or capital gains, depending on the fund structure and your country — and in Germany, accumulating funds are additionally subject to the *Vorabpauschale* (a tax on a deemed annual return, with the base rate set at 3.20% for 2026). In some countries the fund route is slightly more tax-efficient; in others it is worse. The difference is usually small on a cash-like return, but check before moving a large balance. Our [tax guide for foreign bank accounts](/en/blog/tax-guide-foreign-bank-accounts-europe-2026) covers the country-by-country rules.

## Liquidity: Instant vs. 24–48 Hours

A savings account gives you your money instantly, 24/7, including weekends. An MMF sale typically settles in 24–48 hours on business days — sell on Friday afternoon and the money may not arrive until Tuesday. For an emergency fund, that delay is disqualifying. For a house deposit you need in three months, it is irrelevant. Match the liquidity to the purpose.

## Which Should You Choose?

- **Choose a High-Yield Savings Account if:** You want instant 24/7 access to your cash for daily debit card spending and prefer DGS protection up to €100,000.
- **Choose a Money Market Fund if:** You are holding cash balances above €50,000 (exceeding typical neobank interest caps) or want flexible multi-currency cash yields.

### When MMFs make sense

- **Large balances.** Many high-yield savings accounts cap the top rate at €50,000 (or less), paying little or nothing above the cap. An MMF has no cap, so a €200,000 balance earns the full yield on every euro. Above the cap, the MMF's ~1.92% beats a savings account's 0% on the excess — even though it loses to the savings account below the cap.
- **Cash inside a brokerage account.** If you already hold investments with a broker that sweeps idle cash into an MMF, the fund yield is free money on cash that would otherwise earn nothing. Some brokers pay interest on uninvested cash directly instead — compare the two.
- **Multi-currency cash.** USD MMFs yield around 3.59% (7-day average, March 2026, per Crane Data) and GBP MMFs are similarly higher than their euro equivalents, because US and UK policy rates are higher. If you hold dollars or pounds, an MMF in that currency is often the best parking spot.
- **Treasury-style safety without a bank.** If you distrust bank counterparty risk and prefer diversified sovereign and bank paper, an MMF's portfolio structure is the argument — though for balances under €100,000 the DGS makes this a theoretical concern.

### When savings accounts win

- Balances under the interest cap (usually €50,000).
- Emergency funds and any money you might need this week.
- Anyone who wants the €100,000 deposit guarantee.
- Anyone who wants the highest yield available on euro cash right now.

## FAQ

**Can a money market fund lose money?**
Yes, in theory — it is an investment, not a deposit, and its value can fall if the underlying paper defaults or if markets seize up. In practice, EU-regulated MMFs are among the most conservative investments available, with strict diversification and liquidity rules. The risk is very low, but it is not zero, and there is no deposit guarantee.

**Do I need a broker to buy an MMF?**
Usually yes — through a broker, a bank's fund platform, or a fintech app that offers MMF access (several now sweep cash into MMFs automatically). Our guides to the [best ETF brokers in Europe](/en/blog/best-etf-brokers-europe-2026) and the [best low-cost stock brokers](/en/blog/best-low-cost-stock-brokers-europe-2026) cover the main access points.

**Are MMF returns taxed as interest?**
It depends on your country and the fund. In many EU countries, distributing MMFs are taxed similarly to interest, while accumulating MMFs are taxed as capital gains on sale — and Germany applies the Vorabpauschale to both. Check your national rules before investing a large sum.
