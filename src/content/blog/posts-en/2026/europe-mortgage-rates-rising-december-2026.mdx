# Mortgage Rates Are Rising Again in Europe — and December 17 Is the Date That Matters

After two years of falling or stable borrowing costs, mortgages in the eurozone are getting more expensive again. The European Central Bank has raised rates twice in 2026, inflation has climbed back to 4%, and markets now price an [82% chance of another hike](https://watchtowerterminal.com/ecb-preview-december-2026/) at the ECB's final meeting of the year, on 17 December — a [Reuters survey of 71 economists](https://www.fxstreet.com/analysis/the-euro-is-at-its-weakest-since-spring-2025-and-the-ecb-is-about-to-hike-202611171404) taken on 10–12 November found all of them expecting a 25-basis-point rise to 2.75%. Banks have already passed part of the increase on to borrowers — and the next move could add more.

This is not a distant central-bank debate. The average cost of a new mortgage in the eurozone reached 3.62% in September 2026, up from around 3.3% a year earlier, according to ECB statistics cited by [ImmoExus](https://immoexus.com/guides/real-estate-news/mortgage-rates-rising-in-europe). For a buyer signing a loan in the coming weeks, the difference between locking a rate now and waiting until after 17 December could be hundreds of euros a year.

## Why rates are going up again

The trigger was energy. The war involving Iran and the disruption to oil and gas supplies pushed energy prices up sharply in 2026, and with them inflation. In June the ECB raised its deposit rate from 2.00% to 2.25%, its first increase since September 2023. After a pause in July, it raised rates again in September to 2.50%.

On 29 October the ECB kept rates unchanged, but the pressure has not gone away. Eurostat's flash estimate put inflation at 4.0% in October, up from 3.8% in September, with energy prices around a fifth higher than a year earlier. Core inflation, which excludes energy and food, ticked up to 2.6%, according to [FXStreet](https://www.fxstreet.com/analysis/week-ahead-flash-pmis-and-pce-inflation-in-focus-ahead-of-thanksgiving-202611201011). At double the ECB's 2% target, headline inflation is now the number that dominates every policy discussion in Frankfurt.

The hawks on the Governing Council are no longer whispering. Bundesbank President Joachim Nagel said on 20 November that eurozone inflation remains too high and that the ECB is likely to tighten policy at its December meeting, telling German television that "further action may be necessary" after the two hikes already delivered this year, as reported by [Trading Dashboard](https://tradingdashboard.com/en/news/nagel-backs-december-ecb-rate-hike-push). ECB Vice President Boris Vujcic and Chief Economist Philip Lane have made similar noises in recent days. Markets expect the 17 December meeting to deliver a 25-basis-point hike, with two more of the same size anticipated in 2027, taking the deposit rate from 2.50% toward a range of 3.0% to 3.25%.

There is one more decision to watch before that meeting: on 25 November, ECB policymakers will discuss a proposal to raise the minimum reserve requirement imposed on commercial banks from 1% to 2%. If approved, it would tighten lending conditions further, even without a rate move.

## What borrowers are paying now

The rises have already reached the mortgage market, unevenly. Variable-rate loans react fastest: the average rate on eurozone mortgages with a rate fixed for up to one year rose to 3.86% in August. The country-by-country picture, compiled by [ImmoExus](https://immoexus.com/guides/real-estate-news/mortgage-rates-rising-in-europe) from ECB data and national brokers, shows how different the same rate cycle feels in different markets:

| Country | Typical loan | Recent rate | Direction |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| France | 20-year fixed | 3.44% in August, about 3.54% in early September | Rising; brokers expect 3.8% to 4% by end of 2026 |
| Germany | 20-year fixed | Around 4% to 4.5% in August | Expected to move sideways |
| Italy | 20 to 30-year fixed | 3.46% in August | Expected to reach about 3.75% by end of 2026 |
| Italy | Variable | About 2.80% in early September | Expected to rise to about 3.05% |
| Spain | 30-year fixed | About 2.2% to 2.8% | Up from 2% to 2.5% in June; some banks raising offers further |
| Eurozone average | All new mortgages | 3.62% in September | Up from around 3.3% a year earlier |

France is the most exposed of the large economies. Almost all French mortgages are fixed for the whole term, so existing borrowers are protected — but new buyers feel every change, and brokers warn the average 20-year rate could approach 4% by the end of the year. Germany is already there: 20-year fixed rates were around 4% to 4.5% in August, because German mortgage rates follow long-term government bond yields more closely than ECB decisions, and those yields rose earlier in the year.

Spain, by contrast, still has some of the lowest mortgage rates in the eurozone, with 30-year fixed loans offered at roughly 2.2% to 2.8%. But rates have risen since June, and several banks are expected to raise their offers by up to half a percentage point, while others may hold rates to win customers. For Spanish borrowers on variable rates, the squeeze is more immediate: the 12-month Euribor closed on Friday 20 November at 3.372%, just 0.007 points below the year's maximum, and the provisional November average of 3.300% already points to December revisions costing around €97 to €100 more per month on a typical loan, according to [Rankia](https://www.rankia.com/blog/mejores-hipotecas/7562348-euribor-hoy-23-noviembre-2026). We covered the Euribor picture in detail in our [Spanish-language analysis of the December revisions](https://finanzaseu.com/blog/euribor-noviembre-2026-revision-hipoteca-variable).

The best fixed-rate offers in Spain are still competitive for now. A November comparison of more than 100 bank tariffs by OCU, reported by [Euribor.com.es](https://www.euribor.com.es/2026/09/21/mejores-hipotecas-2/), put Openbank's fixed mortgage at the top, with a 2.90% nominal rate and a 3.32% APR, while Coinc led the variable offers with a 3.88% APR. Notably, 64.1% of mortgages signed in Spain in August were fixed-rate — borrowers are already voting with their feet.

## What a higher rate costs you in practice

Even a small change in the rate adds up over a long loan. On a €250,000 mortgage over 25 years, moving from 3% to 4% adds around €134 a month — €1,186 a month at 3% versus €1,320 at 4% — and about €40,000 in interest over the life of the loan, according to calculations cited by [ImmoExus](https://immoexus.com/guides/real-estate-news/mortgage-rates-rising-in-europe).

That arithmetic is why the 17 December decision matters even for buyers who will not sign until 2027. Banks price fixed-rate offers on expectations of future ECB moves, not just on today's rate. If the December hike lands as markets expect, fixed offers will adjust within days — and the cheapest deals available today may not be there in January.

Rising rates have not yet stopped house prices from increasing: Eurostat recorded a 4.7% rise in EU house prices in the year to the second quarter of 2026. Buyers are therefore being squeezed from both sides — paying more for the house and more for the loan. That combination is exactly what makes the next few weeks the worst time to drift without a plan.

## Fixed or variable — and what to do before 17 December

When rates are rising, the case for fixing becomes stronger, but it depends on your situation and on what your bank offers. Long fixed rates are already priced on expectations of further increases, so they are not always much higher than variable ones. A fixed rate gives you a payment that will not change, which matters most if your budget is tight or you plan to keep the loan for many years. A variable rate can start lower and falls when the ECB cuts again, but every increase passes straight into your payment. A mixed loan — fixed for the first years and then variable — is common in Spain and Italy and can be a compromise. And before choosing, check the early repayment rules: in some countries, repaying a fixed loan early is cheap or capped by law; in others it can be expensive.

Five practical steps before the December decision:

1. **Get an approval in principle now**, and ask how long the bank will hold the offered rate. Some banks hold a rate for 30 days, others for 90 — the difference matters if you sign in January.
2. **Compare at least three lenders, or use a broker.** Differences between banks can be larger than the ECB's moves: in Spain, the gap between the best and the average fixed offer is currently more than half a percentage point.
3. **Test your budget at a rate one percentage point higher than today's offer.** If the payment at 4% instead of 3% breaks your budget, the loan is too big or the fix is too short.
4. **If you are close to signing, lock the rate in writing before 17 December.** A verbal quote is not a lock.
5. **If you already have a variable-rate mortgage**, ask your bank whether switching to a fixed rate makes sense — especially in Spain, where the December Euribor revision is about to bite.

## The outlook: higher for longer, with a new president

Much depends on energy prices. If inflation keeps rising, the ECB is likely to raise rates again in December and possibly in early 2027, and mortgage rates in France and Italy would probably continue to climb. If energy prices calm down, rates could stabilise during 2027.

There is also a leadership change coming. President Christine Lagarde has announced she will step down early, in February 2027. Her successor will inherit an inflation rate double the target, a deposit rate that markets expect to reach 3.0% to 3.25% next year, and a housing market where prices keep rising even as borrowing gets more expensive.

For savers, the same rate rises are good news: the deposit rate anchors what banks pay on savings, and the promotional rates near 4% we analysed in our [Spanish-language review of the savings-rate war](https://finanzaseu.com/blog/mejores-cuentas-remuneradas-2026) exist precisely because the ECB rate is high. For borrowers, the message is the opposite. The cheap-money era of 2020–2025 is over, the December meeting is likely to make borrowing more expensive still, and the window to lock today's rates closes on 17 December. If you are buying, do the paperwork now — not in January.
