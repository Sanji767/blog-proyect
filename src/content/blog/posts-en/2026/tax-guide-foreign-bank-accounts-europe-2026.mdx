# Tax on Foreign Bank Accounts in Europe (2026): Interest, Forms & Rules

Holding a bank account or investment account in another EU member state (such as Germany via Trade Republic, Lithuania via Revolut, Belgium via Wise, or the Netherlands via Bunq) is fully protected under EU law on free movement of capital.

However, holding assets across international borders brings specific **tax reporting obligations** that every investor and saver should understand.

In this comprehensive guide, we explain how foreign interest income is taxed, how the automatic exchange of financial information operates under CRS, and how to remain 100% tax compliant.

---

## 1. Tax Reporting on Foreign Interest Income

Most cross-border digital banks (e.g. Trade Republic, Revolut LT) pay interest **gross without deducting local withholding tax at source**.

As a tax resident, you are legally responsible for declaring your total annual gross interest income on your local annual personal income tax return under savings or investment income.

### The core rule: you pay tax where you live, not where the bank is

Your tax residence — usually the country where you spend more than 183 days a year or where your centre of economic interests lies — determines how your interest is taxed. The bank's country is mostly irrelevant to the rate you pay. What changes with a foreign account is *who withholds the tax*: a domestic bank usually deducts it automatically, while a foreign bank usually pays you gross and leaves the declaration to you. The tax is still owed.

### How interest is taxed in the main countries

Rates and allowances differ sharply across Europe. These are the rules for 2026 for the countries most of our readers live in:

| Country | How interest is taxed | Key allowance / note |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Spain** | Progressive savings scale: 19% to €6,000, 21% to €50,000, 23% to €200,000, 27% to €300,000, 30% above | No general allowance on interest |
| **Germany** | Flat 25% + 5.5% solidarity surcharge = **26.375%** | €1,000/year tax-free per person (€2,000 for couples) via Freistellungsauftrag |
| **Netherlands** | Box 3 wealth tax on deemed return, not actual interest | €59,357 tax-free allowance in 2026 |
| **France** | Flat tax (PFU) 31.4% for 2026 income (12.8% income tax + 18.6% social charges) | Option to use progressive scale instead |

**Spain.** Savings income — interest, dividends and capital gains — is taxed on a progressive scale: [19% on the first €6,000, 21% up to €50,000, 23% up to €200,000, 27% up to €300,000 and 30% above that](https://taxsummaries.pwc.com/spain/individual/taxes-on-personal-income), according to PwC's tax summaries. On €1,000 of interest from Trade Republic, a Spanish resident owes €190. The interest goes in the savings base (*base del ahorro*) of your annual *renta* return.

**Germany.** Capital income is taxed at a flat 25% plus a 5.5% solidarity surcharge on the tax, for a combined **26.375%** — but the first €1,000 per person per year (€2,000 for married couples filing jointly) is tax-free under the *Sparerpauschbetrag*. To use it, file a *Freistellungsauftrag* (exemption order) with each bank or broker; you can split the €1,000 across institutions. Without the order, the bank withholds the full 26.375% and you reclaim the difference in your tax return. Note that German brokers also apply the *Vorabpauschale* — a tax on the deemed annual return of funds, even if you did not sell — with the base rate set at 3.20% for 2026, the highest since the rule was introduced. ([Source: expats.de](https://expats.de/en/banking/capital-gains-tax-germany/); [German Banking Association](https://bankenverband.de/en/consumers/understanding-pre-determined-tax-basis-vorabpauschale).)

**Netherlands.** The Dutch system does not tax your actual interest. Instead, Box 3 taxes a *deemed* return on your net wealth above a tax-free allowance — [€59,357 in 2026](https://qonto.com/en-nl/open-an-account). If your total wealth is below the threshold, you pay nothing in Box 3 regardless of the interest you earned. Above it, the deemed return is taxed at 36%. This means a Dutch saver with a modest emergency fund typically owes no tax on its interest at all, while a large saver pays on a deemed figure that may be higher or lower than the interest actually received.

**France.** The *prélèvement forfaitaire unique* (PFU) taxes interest at a flat rate that rose in 2026: the 2026 Social Security Financing Law increased the CSG component, taking total social charges from 17.2% to 18.6% and the [overall PFU to 31.4% (12.8% income tax + 18.6% social charges)](https://www.dlapiperrealworld.com/law/index.html?t=corporate-vehicles&c=FR&s=governance-and-taxation-of-corporate-vehicles&q=taxation) for income received in 2026, up from 30% in 2025. You can opt for the progressive income tax scale instead if your marginal rate is lower — worth checking if your total income is modest.

## 2. Automatic Information Exchange (CRS / DAC2)

Under the **Common Reporting Standard (CRS)** and the EU Directive on Administrative Cooperation ([DAC2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Directive_on_Administrative_Co-operation_in_the_field_of_Taxation_%282011/16%29), which implements CRS inside the EU), financial institutions across all EU member states automatically report your account balances, interest earned, and personal identification tax details to your home tax authority each calendar year.

### What this means in practice

- **Your foreign bank already tells your tax authority.** When you open an account with Trade Republic, Revolut, Wise or N26, you provide your tax identification number and country of tax residence. The bank reports your year-end balance and the interest it paid you to its local authority, which forwards the data to your home authority automatically. There is no action for you to take — and no way to opt out.
- **"They won't find out" stopped being a strategy years ago.** The exchange covers over 100 jurisdictions worldwide, not just the EU. Undeclared foreign interest is one of the easiest things for a tax authority to detect, because the data arrives in a standardised format every year.
- **The exchange is about information, not tax.** CRS does not tax anything; it tells your home authority what you earned. The tax itself is assessed under your home country's rules, as above.

## 3. Extra Declarations: When a Normal Tax Return Is Not Enough

Some countries require separate informational returns for foreign assets, on top of the annual income tax return. Missing these carries fines even when no tax is owed.

### Spain: Modelo 720 (and 721 for crypto)

Spanish tax residents must file **Modelo 720** if their foreign assets exceed **€50,000 in any one of three separate categories**: bank accounts abroad, securities and investments abroad, or real estate abroad. The threshold is per category, not in total — €40,000 in foreign accounts plus €60,000 in a foreign brokerage means you declare the securities category, even though no single account reaches €50,000.

- **Deadline:** 1 January to 31 March of the following year. Assets held at 31 December 2026 are declared by 31 March 2027.
- **Re-filing:** after the first return, you only file again for a category if its value has risen by more than €20,000, or if you disposed of a declared asset.
- **Purely informational:** no tax is paid with the form itself — but omitting it carries fines.
- **History:** in January 2022 the Court of Justice of the EU (case C-788/19) struck down Spain's original penalty regime — including a 150% fine and unlimited back-taxation — as a disproportionate restriction on free movement of capital. The filing obligation survived; the draconian penalties did not.

Cryptoassets held with foreign custodians get their own form, **Modelo 721**, with the same €50,000 threshold. ([Sources: sttld](https://www.sttld.io/spain/tax/modelo-720); [Pomerol](https://www.pomerol.es/modelo-720-form-721-assets-abroad-spain/).)

### Other countries

- **France:** foreign accounts must be declared annually on form 3916, even with a zero balance — no threshold.
- **Italy:** foreign financial assets are declared in the RW section of the tax return, and are subject to IVAFE — [0.2% of value per year on securities and other financial assets, and a flat €34.20 per year on foreign current accounts and savings deposits](https://stripe.com/gb/resources/more/italian-tax-on-financial-assets-owned-abroad).
- **Belgium:** foreign accounts must be reported to the National Bank of Belgium's central register, in addition to being declared in the tax return.
- **Germany:** no separate foreign-asset declaration for most residents — foreign interest is declared in the KAP annex of the tax return, and the Freistellungsauftrag (see above) must be filed with foreign banks too, where they accept it. Trade Republic, as a German broker, handles German withholding directly.

If you live in a country not listed here, check with your national tax authority or a local tax adviser — the trend across the EU is toward more disclosure, not less.

## Practical Checklist for Peace of Mind

1. **Download Annual Tax Certificates:** In January, export your Annual Tax Summary PDF from Trade Republic, Wise, or Revolut.
2. **Report Gross Interest:** Sum your annual interest earnings and enter them into your tax return under investment/savings income.
3. **Verify Local Foreign Asset Thresholds:** Check if your jurisdiction requires mandatory informational declarations for offshore balances exceeding specific thresholds.
4. **File your exemption orders early:** In Germany, set the Freistellungsauftrag at each bank before your interest exceeds €1,000 — it applies retroactively within the year, but setting it in January avoids reclaiming withheld tax later.
5. **Keep records for at least 5 years:** Tax authorities can ask for documentation well after the filing year. Keep the annual summaries, the account statements and any exemption-order confirmations together.

## FAQ

**Do I pay tax twice — once abroad and once at home?**
Usually not. Most foreign digital banks pay interest gross, so there is no foreign withholding to reclaim. If a foreign bank does withhold tax (for example, on dividends), double-taxation treaties generally let you credit the foreign tax against your domestic liability. Keep the withholding certificate.

**I earned €50 of interest. Do I really have to declare it?**
Yes. There is no de minimis threshold for declaring interest income in most countries — the €1,000 German allowance and the €59,357 Dutch threshold are exceptions, and even there the income is declared and then exempted, not ignored.

**Does moving country change anything?**
Yes, fundamentally. Tax residence follows where you live, not your nationality. If you move mid-year, you may owe tax in two countries for different parts of the year, and the split-year rules vary. Get advice before the move, not after.
