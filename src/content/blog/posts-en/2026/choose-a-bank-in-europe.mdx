# How to choose a bank in Europe (without overthinking)

Picking a bank is rarely about “the best bank”. It’s about **the best fit for your use case**: salary, travel, multi‑currency, invoices, savings, or just an account that doesn’t get in the way.

This quick guide is a checklist you can reuse whenever you compare options.

## 1. Start with the basics: license and deposit protection

Before features, check the boring (but important) stuff:

- **License / regulator** (who supervises the bank).
- **Deposit protection** (when it applies, and under which entity).
- **Where the IBAN is issued** (and whether that matters for your context).

If you can’t verify these quickly on the official site, that’s a red flag.

### Bank vs. e-money institution: the distinction that matters

Not every app that looks like a bank is one. A licensed bank (N26, bunq, Revolut Bank UAB) holds your deposits on its own balance sheet and is covered by a deposit guarantee scheme. An e-money institution (Wise, for example) is not a bank: it must safeguard your money in segregated accounts at partner banks, but your balance is not covered by a deposit guarantee scheme in the same way.

For a current account holding a few hundred euros, the difference is minor. For your salary account or your emergency fund, it is not: choose a licensed bank. Under [Directive 2014/49/EU](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/en/LSU/?uri=CELEX:32014L0049), deposits at EU banks are protected up to **€100,000 per depositor per bank**, with repayment within 7 working days since 2024. The limit applies per depositor per bank — so a joint account covers each holder up to €100,000, and money spread across two banks is covered up to €100,000 at each.

Two things to verify on any bank's site before you open an account:

1. **Which country's scheme covers you.** It is the country of the bank's licence, not the country you live in. A Lithuanian-licensed bank is covered by the Lithuanian scheme; a German-licensed bank by the German scheme. The €100,000 limit is the same everywhere in the EU, but the authority you would claim from differs.
2. **Whether the entity you are signing up with is the bank itself.** Some fintechs operate through partner banks or hold an e-money licence in one country and a banking licence in another. The footer of the signup page usually says which entity provides the account — read it.

## 2. IBAN and SEPA: what matters in practice

For most people, “IBAN” is just the account number. In practice, it can affect:

- Salary payments (some employers have restrictions).
- Direct debits (utilities, subscriptions).
- International transfers and bank‑to‑bank compatibility.

If you move around Europe, a **SEPA‑friendly setup** makes your life easier.

SEPA now covers [41 countries](https://www.tagada.io/glossary/sepa), and under EU law every euro IBAN must be treated equally — a Lithuanian or Belgian IBAN is legally as valid as a German one for salary, rent and direct debits. In practice, some employers, landlords and utilities still refuse non-local IBANs. That refusal is illegal under Article 9 of the SEPA Regulation, but fighting it takes time and rent is due this month. Our guide to [IBAN discrimination in the EU](/en/blog/iban-discrimination-eu-consumer-rights-2026) explains your rights and the complaint process step by step — including the fact that the European Court of Auditors found in 2025 that the problem persists, with France and Spain accounting for the largest shares of reported cases.

The practical fix is to check which IBAN a bank issues *to residents of your country* before you open the account:

- **N26** issues a Spanish IBAN to Spanish residents and a German IBAN to German residents.
- **bunq** issues a Dutch IBAN.
- **Revolut** issues local IBANs in several countries and a Lithuanian IBAN elsewhere.
- **Wise** issues a Belgian IBAN for euro balances.

If a local IBAN matters for your salary or rent, pick a bank that issues one where you live — or run two free accounts, one local and one international. That two-account setup is the recommendation in most of our guides for a reason: it costs nothing and it removes the IBAN problem entirely.

### Instant payments: free is now the rule

Since the EU's Instant Payments Regulation took effect, euro instant transfers must cost no more than standard transfers — and for most banks that means free. When comparing banks, check that SEPA Instant is actually enabled on the free plan, not locked behind a paid tier. A bank that charges for instant transfers in 2026 is behind the market.

## 3. Fees that actually impact you

Ignore marketing words and look for the fees that hit in real life:

- Monthly plan cost (if any).
- Card fees (physical, replacement, FX markup).
- ATM withdrawal limits and costs.
- Exchange rates for card payments (especially while travelling).

The fee that catches most people is the one they never see: the **FX markup**. A bank that advertises "0% commission" on currency exchange may still apply a 1–2% markup to the exchange rate itself. On €5,000 of annual foreign spending, a 1.5% markup costs €75 a year — more than most paid plans. If you travel or spend in other currencies regularly, compare the actual rate in the app against the mid-market rate, or use a multi-currency account. Our comparison of the [best multi-currency accounts in Europe](/en/blog/best-multi-currency-accounts-europe-2026) and the [best no-FX-fee travel cards](/en/blog/best-no-fx-fee-travel-cards-2026) does that comparison for you.

Other fees worth checking before you commit:

- **ATM limits.** Free plans typically allow €200–€400 a month in free ATM withdrawals, then charge 1–2%. If you use cash often, this matters more than the monthly fee.
- **Card replacement.** A lost physical card typically costs €5–€10 to replace. Virtual cards are usually free and instant — see our guide to [disposable virtual cards](/en/blog/best-disposable-virtual-cards-2026).
- **Paid-plan traps.** A €9.99/month plan is €120 a year. Before upgrading, check whether you actually use the benefits — most people do not need travel insurance, airport lounges or metal cards, and the free plan already covers the basics.

### What about interest on your balance?

The [ECB deposit facility rate is 2.50%](https://www.ecb.europa.eu/press/pr/date/2026/html/ecb.mp261029~9a493adf17.en.html) (held at the 29 October 2026 meeting; next decision 17 December 2026), and the best savings accounts pass most of it on — Trade Republic pays 2.50% on cash balances, for example. But most current accounts pay nothing on your balance. Do not choose a current account for its interest rate; choose it for fees, IBAN and usability, and keep savings in a dedicated high-yield account. Our [best high-yield savings accounts](/en/blog/best-high-yield-savings-accounts-europe-2026) guide covers the options, and our [money market funds vs. savings accounts](/en/blog/money-market-funds-vs-savings-accounts-2026) comparison covers the alternative for larger balances.

## 4. A simple decision framework

Use a “primary + support” approach:

1) Choose one account as your **main** (salary, bills, stability).
2) Add a **secondary** one only if you truly need it (travel, multi‑currency, business).

That’s usually better than forcing one bank to do everything.

### The checklist, in order

Work through these questions in order. The first account that answers yes to all of them is your main account:

1. **Is it a licensed bank with €100,000 deposit protection?** If no, it is a secondary account at best.
2. **Does it issue an IBAN your employer and landlord will accept?** If a local IBAN matters, this is a hard filter.
3. **Is the free plan genuinely free?** No monthly fee, no minimum balance, free SEPA transfers, free instant payments.
4. **Are the fees you will actually pay low?** Check ATM limits and FX markup against your real usage, not the marketing page.
5. **Does the app do what you need?** Instant notifications, card freeze, virtual cards, and sub-accounts (Spaces/pockets) for budgeting are the features that matter day to day. Everything else is decoration.
6. **Can you reach a human?** Check support hours and channels before you need them. In-app chat with long queues is fine for a secondary account; for your main account, you want a phone number.

### Common combinations that work

- **Living in one country, rarely travelling:** one local-IBAN bank (N26 or bunq) as your main account. Done.
- **Expat or frequent traveller:** local-IBAN bank for salary and bills + Revolut or Wise for multi-currency spending and transfers.
- **Freelancer:** a personal account plus a separate free business account — N26 Business and bunq Free Business both cost €0 a month. See our guide to the [best free business accounts for freelancers](/en/blog/best-free-business-accounts-freelancers-2026).
- **Saver:** current account for daily life + a high-yield savings account for the emergency fund. Never mix the two.
